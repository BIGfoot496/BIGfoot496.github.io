## A paper a day: day 18

Today's [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.13677) is a rare case of me choosing a paper to read because of the person who wrote it. I've had a bit of a... professional crush? on [Dr Becky Smethurst](https://www.youtube.com/drbecky) since high school. Today after watching (still can't believe my luck that they now have video) the newest episode of [The Supermassive Podcast](https://www.youtube.com/@TheSupermassivePod) I realised I've never actually read any of Dr Becky's papers, and thought it was time to remedy this omission. 

Sooooo, this paper was hard for me \**awkward laugh*\*. Turns out just watching explainers on the topic, even the very best ones, is not enough to fully grasp what's written in papers, who'd've thunk... 

What I got was this: there is a well-known correlation between how massive a galaxy's stars are and how supermassive is the black hole in its centre. And similar correlations exist between SMBH mass and other parameters, like stellar velocities or bulge sizes. But astrophysicists still argue why these correlations appear. The prevailing hypothesis for a time was that the growth of both the black holes and their galaxies was governed by mergers. Something along the lines of "a galaxy merger redistributes angular momentum and feeds both star formation and black hole accretion." This paper is yet another piece of evidence against the view that the mergers are the only thing that matters. The authors used a (standard?) simulation, which accounts for, frankly, staggering amount of astrophysical phenomena, to get data on ~6800 simulated galaxies. They extracted the ones that had not experienced a merger since redshift 2 (it tickles me how it's a measure of both distance and time) and the ones that had undergone more than three mergers since then. And then they basically just compared these groups for the correlations between black hole and galaxy evolution. Turned out the galaxies, which have never merged with anyone and kept to themselves their whole life, still had the same-ish correlations with their supermassive black holes as the social butterflies that merged a bunch. Mergers do make the correlations stronger and reduce the scatter, but without them the correlations still persist.
