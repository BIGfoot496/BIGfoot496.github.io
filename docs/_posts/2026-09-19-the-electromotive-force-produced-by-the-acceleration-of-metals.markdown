## A paper a day: day 8
I missed an entire week, but I have a good excuse: my laptop developed a rapidly progressing dementia just as I was writing a post, and I had literally no way to post anything for a while. I'm back, and hopefully won't have such technical issues again any time soon. 

Today's [paper](https://cref.if.ufrgs.br/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/stewart-tolman.pdf) I actually read several days ago, while preparing for the PhD program entrance exams. Very cool experiment, wonder why it isn't taught in high school physics. Imagine, you're in a time before it was known for certain electrons are what carries charge in metals. Electron itself is known, but not whether the charge carrier is that or something else. How do you test whether it is electrons? Mandelstam & Papalexi (whose paper I could not find, afaict their experiment was never written up as a proper paper), and then Tolman & Stewart (making the experiment quantitative) decided to use the fact that electrons have a known mass. They quickly rotated a copper coil, and then abrubptly stopped it. But electrons, due to intertia, _continued moving_. Mandelstam & Papalexi had a phone attached to their coil, and they heard a sound. Tolman & Stewart had a ballistic galvanometer, so they actually measured the charge that flowed through the coil, and thus measured the charge carrier's mass (mass to charge ratio, actually, but never mind). It turned out to be pretty dang close to the mass of an electron in a vacuum. Really surprised it actually worked. They had to get rid of _the Earth's magnetic field_ for that, for crying out loud... But it WORKED!
